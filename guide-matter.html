States of matter and particle models

Solids, liquids and gases behave differently because their particles are arranged differently and move in different ways. The particle model helps explain visible changes without assuming that individual particles grow or shrink.

Compare the three states

  • Solid: particles remain close together and vibrate about positions. A solid usually keeps its shape and volume.
  • Liquid: particles remain close together but move past one another. A liquid keeps approximately the same volume and takes the shape of its container.
  • Gas: particles move freely and are usually far apart relative to their size. A gas spreads to fill the available space.

Changes of state

Melting changes a solid into a liquid; freezing changes a liquid into a solid. Evaporation occurs at a liquid surface and can happen below the boiling point; boiling occurs throughout the liquid at its boiling temperature under a given pressure. Condensation changes a gas into a liquid.

Worked example

Water droplets form on the outside of a cold glass. The water usually comes from water vapour in the surrounding air condensing on the cool surface—not from water passing through the glass.

Temperature and energy

Heating typically increases the average kinetic energy of particles. During a change of state, supplied or released energy can change particle arrangements rather than simply raising or lowering temperature.

Check your understanding

1. Does evaporation happen only when water boils?

Show explanationNo. Evaporation can occur at the surface of liquid water at temperatures below its boiling point.

2. What happens to the particles when ice melts?

Show explanationThe water particles do not change identity; their movement and arrangement change as the solid becomes liquid.
